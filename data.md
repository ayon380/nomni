Releasing decern: a deterministic authorisation kernel for agentic systems.

An agent does not borrow your credentials. It exercises your authority, thousands of times, through chains you did not author, at hours when you are not awake to be asked. Access control was built for a person at the other end. None of that survives contact with an agent.

Two properties are the whole point.

Authority you can prove. A decision is a pure function of principal, authority graph, policy and time, which makes it small enough for cvc5 to check nine safety invariants across the modelled input domain rather than the handful a test file covers. Each invariant ships with a negative control that fails if the guarantee is removed, so a green run is evidence the proofs are load-bearing.

A record you can verify. Append-only, Ed25519-signed, hash-chained. A decision is served only if its record was written first. If it cannot be recorded, you get a 503, never a bare allow.

What it does not do is name the party the decision was about, because the vocabulary it implements has no slot for them. That gap is the subject of two Internet-Drafts I filed during IETF 126.

Over the past few months I've been writing about one shift in engineering.

Coding agents work because they run inside symbolic systems. Compilers, tests and execution environments can prove them wrong.

Then I argued that the unit of engineering has been climbing. From prompts, to context, to harnesses, to loops.

Reading Bun's migration from Zig to Rust reinforced that progression. The interesting part was not the language. It was that the workflow had become the thing being engineered.

While writing this essay I realised something sits underneath all of it.

Those loops work because they have an external witness.

The compiler writes down what happened, and it survives the iteration. The agent does not.

The witness disappears the moment loops compose across agents, services and institutions.

Inside one loop, it comes almost for free.

At the boundary between loops, there is no compiler.

The next engineering problem is no longer making a single reasoning loop work.

It is making many reasoning loops accountable to one another.

That is the question behind The Compiler Forgives. The Loop Forgets.

This LinkedIn article is an abridged version. The complete essay is linked in the first comment.

Evidence is not what a system records. It is what survives what the system does next.

I submitted two Internet-Drafts to the #IETF this week before #IETF126

#OAuth and #JWT records model who acts (act) and who delegated (sub). Two facts still have no field: who a decision is about, and whether a human or an agent performed the action.

draft-aravind-oauth-decision-subject defines dsub. It names the decision subject: the party a decision is about, when that party is not the delegator, not the agent, and not the resource owner. An agent screens an applicant. The record of that decision cannot name the applicant. dsub is that field.

draft-aravind-oauth-operator-of-record defines opr. It records whether a human or an agent operated. An agent driving a wallet produces a key-binding proof identical to a human-operated one. The proof shows where the key lives, not who exercised it. opr records the difference.

Both claims are descriptive. A policy decision point MUST ignore them. They grant nothing and change no authorization decision. They compose with act and may_act.

Registration path: JWT Claims Registry, Expert Review under RFC 7519 Section 10.1.

Both drafts are on the IETF Datatracker. Links in the first comment.

Review welcome, on-list or here.

This #Layer8 issue looks like it's about #wallets. It isn't.
 
Wallets are simply where a much deeper architectural shift first becomes impossible to ignore.
 
For years, digital #identity rested on a simple model. An issuer created a credential. A holder presented it. A verifier checked it. The protocol established a claim, and the transaction ended there.
 
That simplicity is disappearing.
 
As AI agents begin acting on our behalf, credentials are delegated across devices, and identities travel across borders, presentation is no longer the end of the protocol. It is the start of a decision whose consequences unfold elsewhere.

When presentation and consequence become separable, protocols can no longer preserve only proof of presentation. They must also preserve evidence of consequence.

The protocol is no longer just proving a claim. It is becoming part of the causal chain that produces legally and economically significant outcomes. The question is whether it preserves enough evidence for those outcomes to be attributed, reviewed, and corrected.
 
That is why this is no longer just a technology story. It is a question of digital sovereignty, diplomacy, and governance. Europe is defining much of the legal and architectural foundation through the European Digital Identity Wallet. India is demonstrating what population-scale deployment looks like through Digital Public Infrastructure. As initiatives such as the Ministry of External Affairs' cross-border digital credential work mature, identity protocols are evolving into instruments of international trust, not merely domestic authentication.
 
India already shows how quickly this shift is arriving. National identity now sits beside payment cards, airline boarding passes, and loyalty programmes in the same wallet. Soon, the entity presenting that identity may not be its human holder, but an authorised agent acting within delegated permissions.

Interoperability without addressability scales verification faster than it scales accountability.

This issue of Layer 8 follows that arc. From wallets to delegated identity. From credentials to agents. From authentication protocols to governance infrastructure.
 
The most important change is not that our identity has moved into a wallet.
 
It is that the next hand presenting it may no longer be ours.

Careful reporting by Ajinkya K. in Business Standard on National Payments Corporation Of India (NPCI)'s proposed Unified Agent Protocol. It lands on the design line most coverage skips: NPCI's role stops at confirming a payment request is genuine and the agent is trusted.

The story says industry expects existing dispute machinery to carry over. Worth being precise about what that machinery is. #UPI's chargeback is not a dispute about intent. It is an adjustment raised in #URCS, the clearing and settlement system, processed against the beneficiary bank's response in the next settlement cycle and closing on deemed acceptance if nobody answers. A settlement reconciliation instrument, bank to bank, for money that failed to move, moved twice, or moved to the wrong place. It never had to reason about whether the payment was the one the payer meant, because a human checked the payee and the amount before approving.

#UPICircle shows how #NPCI thinks about delegation. Even under full delegation, capped at ₹5,000 per transaction and ₹15,000 a month, the secondary user must still authorise with a biometric or app passcode, and the primary user sees every transaction. The delegate is capped, watched, and bound to a person at the moment of payment. That binding is the guardrail.

An agent has no biometric. The one guardrail the delegation model depends on is precisely the one that cannot be carried across.

What replaces it changes the record. A verified agent under a valid mandate produces a payment that is authorised by construction. The registry says the agent was legitimate. The mandate says the authority was real. So a payment the user never intended cannot be classified as unauthorised, because on the rail's own evidence it was authorised. It did not fail. It did not go to the wrong beneficiary. There is no adjustment for it. It is simply final.
A trusted-agent registry can attest that an agent was legitimate. It cannot attest that the agent did what it was told. Only the first is being built.

And there was never an economic reason to build the second. UPI runs at #zeroMDR, so there is no per-transaction revenue funding the dispute and recourse infrastructure that card networks pay for out of interchange. Not my inference: in its submission to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance, the Department of Financial Services said the absence of MDR makes the UPI ecosystem financially unsustainable, with government incentives covering roughly 11% of industry costs. Among the underfunded categories named: fraud control and dispute resolution.

So, a payments question. On a final push rail, what happens when a correctly authorised payment was not the one the user meant, where in the protocol does that live, and who pays for it?

Cheap at draft stage. Expensive at 22 billion transactions a month. Publish the spec and consult before the regulatory nod, not after.
